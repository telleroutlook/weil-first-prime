\title{When the Pilot Audits the Tool: \\
       Two Verification Blind Spots Exposed and Closed by a \\
       Real Computational-Mathematics Proof}
\author{Lin Tao}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Proof-orchestration systems promise that a machine-checked release gate makes a
computational proof trustworthy. But a gate only checks the properties it was
told to check. We report a case in which a genuine research proof --- the
FP-0.35 conjecture on positivity of the Weil quadratic form at $L=7/20$ --- acted
as an adversarial pilot against its own verification layer, \proofctl, and
exposed two \emph{kernel blind spots} that every prior release had silently
passed: (i) a generator that merely \emph{copied} a stored certificate while its
attestation claimed from-scratch recomputation, and (ii) a checker that
recomputed honestly but \emph{omitted a term} of the quantity it claimed to
verify, emitting a false pass with a $\sim\!16\times$-inflated margin (a factor of
$15.7$, from $0.01494$ reported vs.\ $0.00095$ true). Neither is
caught by digest-binding, schema validation, or ``all obligations pass'':
the copy-generator produces byte-identical replays, and the omitted-term checker
is internally consistent. We describe the two release conditions added in
response --- \CtenA{} (no copy-only generator) and \CelevenA{} (checker mutation
coverage) --- the epistemic discipline (a written ``proof constitution'') that
the same incident forced, and the closeout in which the corrected pilot was
driven back through the hardened gate. A second, independent instance of the
same blind-spot class (a dependency lemma whose checker also lacked mutation
coverage) surfaced during closeout, confirming the conditions generalise beyond
the originating bug. The broader lesson is methodological: the highest-value
feedback a real proof can give a verification system is not ``it verified'' but
``here is a true statement your gate accepted for the wrong reason.''
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Computational proofs increasingly combine heterogeneous evidence: formal proofs
in proof assistants, certified interval arithmetic, and independently replayable
numerical certificates. A proof-orchestration layer stitches these into one
artefact and gates its release on machine-checkable conditions. \proofctl{} is
such a layer: an open-source Go tool that binds every attestation to the exact
digests of its checker, schema, and dependencies, derives claim acceptance
solely from a checker's \texttt{obligation\_results} (never from a self-reported
\texttt{outcome} field), and blocks release until a set of conditions
\textsf{C01}--\textsf{C11} all pass against the full attestation graph.

The comfortable assumption is that once such a gate is green, the computational
proof is sound up to the mathematics of the individual checkers. This paper is
about the gap in that assumption. A green gate certifies only what its conditions
encode; a property nobody thought to encode can be violated by every release and
never noticed.

Our evidence is concrete. During the development of a real research proof ---
FP-0.35, the assertion that the local Weil quadratic form on
$L^2(-7/20,\,7/20)$ has strictly positive infimum \cite{WFP2026} --- the proof's
own certification exposed two such properties, each violated by a certificate
that \proofctl{} had been happily accepting:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Copy-as-recomputation.} The main certificate's ``generator'' was a
  one-line file copy,
  \begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
  python3 -c "shutil.copy('certs/thm-fp-035.json', \{cert\})"
  \end{quote}
  tagged \texttt{replay\_mode = from\_scratch}. Every replay reproduced the
  stored bytes exactly, so digest-comparison passed. The attestation claimed the
  result was recomputed; it was transcribed.
\item \textbf{Omitted-term checker.} The checker reported all obligations
  \texttt{pass} while computing the second-moment matrix $S^{(0)}$ as its
  $S_{KK}$ block alone, silently dropping the three positive blocks
  $S_{VV}+S_{VK}+S_{KV}$. Dropping positive mass shrinks the Schur residual and
  \emph{inflates} the certified eigenvalue --- here by a factor of $15.7$
  ($0.01494$ reported vs.\ $0.00095$ true). The checker was internally
  consistent and passed its own schema; it was simply verifying a different,
  easier quantity than the one it claimed.
\end{itemize}

Crucially, and this is the subtlety that makes the case worth reporting, the
\emph{conclusion} was correct throughout: $\lambda(7/20)>0$ holds; both defects
are process defects, not sign errors. A verification system that only ever asks
``is the answer right?'' would see nothing wrong. The value of the pilot was to
force the question ``was the answer obtained the way the attestation says it
was?'' --- and the answer was no, twice.

The remainder of the paper describes \proofctl{}'s architecture
(\Cref{sec:arch}), the FP-0.35 proof graph it orchestrates (\Cref{sec:fp035}),
the two blind spots and the conditions \CtenA{}/\CelevenA{} added to close them
(\Cref{sec:blindspots}), the mutation-catalog design that operationalises
\CelevenA{} together with a nuance that naive mutation testing gets wrong
(\Cref{sec:mutation}), the epistemic discipline the incident forced
(\Cref{sec:constitution}), and the closeout in which the corrected pilot passed
the hardened gate and a second instance of the blind-spot class was found and
fixed (\Cref{sec:closeout}).

\section{Architecture of \texorpdfstring{\protect\proofctl}{proofctl}}
\label{sec:arch}

\subsection{ProofGraph and claims}
A \emph{ProofGraph} is a directed acyclic graph of \emph{claims}. Each claim
carries a \texttt{statement\_digest} (SHA-256 of its text), a list of
\texttt{dependencies} with required assurance levels, a \texttt{checker\_policy}
naming the checker that verifies it, and an \texttt{obligations} list specifying
exactly what that checker must certify.

\subsection{Checker protocol}
A checker is a subprocess that receives a certificate path and emits JSON:
\begin{verbatim}
{ "protocol_version": 2,
  "obligation_results": [ {"id": "...", "verdict": "pass"}, ... ],
  "metadata": { "format_version": "...", ... } }
\end{verbatim}
with exit codes $0$ (certified), $1$ (uncertified), $2$ (malformed),
$3$ (resource/blocked). Acceptance is \emph{derived} by \proofctl{} from the
verdict set; a checker cannot assert its own acceptance. The obligation set is
matched \emph{exactly} against the contract: a missing, extra, or duplicated
obligation is a hard reject, so a checker cannot quietly shrink what it is
answerable for.

\subsection{Identity closure and staleness}
Every attestation binds the full identity closure: statement digest, dependency
digests, checker digest, and schema digest, recomputed and re-verified on load.
Any change to an input automatically marks downstream attestations
\emph{stale}, forcing re-verification rather than trusting a cached verdict.

\subsection{Release gate}
The gate evaluates conditions \textsf{C01}--\textsf{C11} against the whole graph.
Representatives: \textsf{C01} every claim accepted (derived from obligation
results, not from a writable \texttt{outcome}); \textsf{C04} replay consistency;
\textsf{C09} no development-only (\texttt{native}) runtime on a releasable claim.
The two conditions this paper is about, \CtenA{} and \CelevenA{}, are
latecomers: they exist because the pilot showed that \textsf{C01}--\textsf{C09}
were jointly insufficient.

\section{The pilot: FP-0.35}
\label{sec:fp035}

FP-0.35 asserts $\lambda(7/20) := \inf_{v\neq0} Q_W^{7/20}(v)/\|v\|^2 > 0$, a
finite-scale positivity statement (it does \emph{not} imply the Riemann
Hypothesis). The proof reduces, via a split-residual Schur criterion, to positive
definiteness of a matrix $C = b_L F - R_\eta$ in each of two parity sectors, where
$R_\eta$ is built from the full second moment
$S^{(0)} = S_{VV}+S_{VK}+S_{KV}+S_{KK}$, the certified Weil constant
$c_L \approx 1.36527$, and prime-shift matrices. The orchestrated proof graph
has 17 inter-dependent claims spanning three evidence regimes: pure rational
arithmetic (window and absorption lemmas), Lean~4 formalisation of the integer
skeleton, and 256-bit Arb interval arithmetic for the archimedean integrals.

This heterogeneity is why orchestration is needed --- and why the blind spots
matter. The main claim depends on two sector lemmas, each of which depends on an
archimedean base certificate of $2.4$\,MB whose re-verification takes tens of
minutes. Nobody re-derives such a chain by hand; the entire point of the gate is
to make its acceptance trustworthy without doing so.

\section{Two blind spots}
\label{sec:blindspots}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\CtenA}{C10}: copy masquerading as recomputation}

\begin{principle}[Recomputation must recompute]
An attestation that claims from-scratch recomputation must run a generator that
performs computation, not a file copy. A byte-exact replay of a copy proves only
that copying is deterministic.
\end{principle}

The failure mode is invisible to digest binding precisely because it is
\emph{too} reproducible. \proofctl{}'s replay compares the generated certificate's
digest against the stored evidence; a \texttt{shutil.copy} reproduces the stored
bytes perfectly, so the comparison always passes. The attestation's
\texttt{replay\_mode = from\_scratch} is a self-report the gate had been trusting.

\CtenA{} closes this by inspecting the recorded \texttt{generator\_cmds} of every
attestation that \emph{claims} substantive recomputation (\texttt{replay\_mode =
from\_scratch}, or a reproducible/exact-replay assurance) and rejecting it when
the generator is purely a copy/no-op primitive
(\texttt{shutil.copy}, \texttt{cp}, \texttt{cat}, \texttt{ln}, \dots) with no
sign of computation. The check is deliberately conservative --- a generator that
imports \texttt{shutil} \emph{and} calls \texttt{recompute}/\texttt{integrate}/
\texttt{schur} is not flagged --- so it blocks the fraud pattern without
penalising legitimate generators that happen to touch the filesystem.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\CelevenA}{C11}: honest recomputation of the wrong quantity}

\CtenA{} is necessary but not sufficient. A checker can genuinely compute --- no
copy anywhere --- and still emit a false pass by computing an \emph{incomplete}
version of the asserted quantity. That is exactly the omitted-term bug: the
generator computed $S^{(0)} = S_{KK}$ only, a real computation, dropping the
three other positive second-moment blocks. The result is a smaller residual, a
larger (inflated) minimum eigenvalue, and a confidently-passed obligation.

\begin{principle}[Sensitivity is verification]
A checker that claims to verify a quantity assembled from terms
$T_1,\dots,T_m$ must be demonstrably sensitive to each $T_i$: perturbing $T_i$
must be capable of changing the verdict. A term whose perturbation never moves
the verdict is a term the checker does not actually check.
\end{principle}

\CelevenA{} operationalises this: any attestation claiming substantive
recomputation must carry, in its metadata, \texttt{mutation\_kill\_rate}
$=$ \texttt{"100\%"} and a non-empty \texttt{mutation\_catalog\_digest} pinning
which mutants were run. The kill rate must be exactly $100\%$: a checker that
lets any catalogued mutant survive has a blind spot of the same class as the
omitted-term bug. \CelevenA{} cannot be satisfied by a certificate-reading
checker that trusts supplied numbers; it forces a recompute-style checker whose
verdict genuinely depends on every asserted term.

\begin{remark}[Why digests and schemas miss both]
Digest binding proves the same bytes were checked by the same script; it says
nothing about whether the script computes the claimed quantity. Fail-closed
schema validation rejects unknown or malformed fields; both defective
certificates were schema-valid. ``All obligations pass'' is exactly the output a
hard-coded or omitted-term checker produces. The two blind spots live in the
space between ``the artefact is well-formed and stably reproduced'' and ``the
artefact means what it claims.''
\end{remark}

\section{Mutation catalogs, and a nuance}
\label{sec:mutation}

To satisfy \CelevenA{}, each recompute checker ships a \emph{mutation catalog}: a
list of single-term perturbations of the assembled matrices, each re-judged by
the \emph{same} assembly and positivity judge the checker uses (no duplicated
logic), recording whether the perturbation ``kills'' the verdict. For FP-0.35 the
catalog has six mutants per sector --- e.g.\ zero the second moment $S^{(0)}$
(the retired-bug class), flip the sign of the coupling diagonal, remove the
kinetic coercivity, scale a prime-coupling block --- and achieves $6/6$ kills.

A subtlety, easy to get wrong, deserves emphasis, because getting it wrong
reintroduces the very failure \CelevenA{} guards against:

\begin{principle}[Kill criterion must match a term's true influence]
A mutant that zeros a term whose genuine influence is negligible will not flip
the verdict --- and this does \emph{not} mean the checker is blind to that term.
Classify mutants. \emph{Judge-sensitivity} mutants (sign flips, zeroing a
dominant term, swapping the positivity judge) must flip the verdict. But a term
with $\sim\!10^{-4}$ influence, zeroed, should be probed by a \emph{large-factor
scaling} mutant that provably moves the pivot, or by a relative-tolerance
threshold --- not by demanding a sign flip it cannot cause.
\end{principle}

Concretely, in the even sector zeroing the prime self-second-moment $S^{(2)}$
moves the minimum pivot from $+0.00870$ to $+0.00871$ --- a $\sim\!10^{-4}$
change. A naive ``100\% kill or the checker is blind'' rule would wrongly flag
the checker as insensitive to $S^{(2)}$. In truth $S^{(2)}$ barely influences the
even sector; the correct probe is a $100\times$ scaling mutant, which drives the
pivot sharply negative and is cleanly killed. The catalog therefore separates
\emph{judge} mutants (must flip) from \emph{recompute-verification} mutants (must
move measurably), each scored by an appropriate criterion.

\section{The discipline the incident forced}
\label{sec:constitution}

The two blind spots were the tool's failure. But the same night exposed a
\emph{human} failure mode that no gate can catch, and that we found worth
codifying as an executable-where-possible ``proof constitution.'' We record the
parts most relevant to anyone building or trusting such a system.

\begin{principle}[Narrative resistance]
\label{prin:narrative}
A computed number that supports a dramatic story --- ``we found a fatal bug,''
``the proof fails'' --- demands \emph{more} scrutiny, not less. Excitement is a
bias multiplier, not evidence.
\end{principle}

During diagnosis, an ad-hoc script produced a minimum pivot of $-0.043$
(``FP-0.35 fails!''), which was accepted three times because it fit the exciting
``we found a fatal bug'' narrative. It was a bug in that script's residual
assembly; the true value was $+0.0087$ all along, and downstream conclusions had
to be retracted. The corrective was mechanical, not attitudinal:

\begin{principle}[Diff artefacts before narrating a disagreement]
When two implementations give different scalars, dump and diff the full
intermediate \emph{matrices} element-wise to locate the exact divergence before
explaining ``why.'' A scalar mismatch plus a plausible story is not a diagnosis.
\end{principle}

The element-wise comparison of the two independent Schur assemblies settled it in
one step: the matrices agreed to $\max|C_A-C_B| = 4\times10^{-3}$, both yielding
$+0.0087$; the $-0.043$ existed in no correct computation.

\begin{principle}[Process defect $\neq$ wrong conclusion]
A defective process (copy generation, omitted term, hard-coded verdict) does not
automatically make the conclusion wrong. State precisely which is broken.
\end{principle}

This principle is what let the pilot be both a genuine bug report against the
tool and a preserved-correct mathematical result: the certificate's process was
doubly broken, yet $\lambda(7/20)>0$ was never in question. Conflating the two
would have either discarded a true theorem or excused a broken pipeline; keeping
them separate is what made \CtenA{}/\CelevenA{} the right response rather than a
retraction.

A last principle bears directly on why \CelevenA{} takes the form it does:

\begin{principle}[Prefer recomputation over certificate-reading]
A checker that recomputes the claim from primitives is structurally immune to
copy-certificate and self-attestation attacks, unlike one that reads and
validates a supplied certificate. Recompute checkers still need mutation coverage
for omitted-term bugs --- but they cannot be fooled by a doctored certificate.
\end{principle}

A third principle, less immediately about tools, emerged from the same campaign
when analysing the range of $\lambda(L)$ across $L \in [7/20, 0.45]$:

\begin{principle}[Certified lower bound $\neq$ true eigenvalue]
\label{prin:lb-vs-true}
A certificate's rigorous lower bound $\lambda_{lb}$ on $\lambda_{\min}$ can
collapse by orders of magnitude while $\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{true}}$ remains
positive --- a phenomenon we call \emph{Certificate Relaxation} (the
certificate becomes loose, not the matrix ill-posed).
Before issuing any verdict of ``collapse'', ``failure'', or ``loss of positive
definiteness'', simultaneously compute (a) the certified lower bound $\lambda_{lb}$
and (b) the direct float minimum eigenvalue $\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{true}}$.
A ratio $\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{true}}/\lambda_{lb} > 100$ strongly suggests
relaxation; a ratio $> 1000$ suspends the verdict until $\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{true}}$
itself confirms the sign change.
\end{principle}

\noindent The incident: at $L=0.42$, the certified lower bound fell to
$\lambda_{lb} = 9.3\times10^{-10}$ (a $10^6\times$ drop from $L=7/20$),
which was interpreted as evidence of ``exponential collapse'' and drove several
downstream strategic decisions.  Direct measurement gave
$\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{true}} = +3.19\times10^{-5}$ --- still positive,
ratio $\approx 34{,}000\times$.  The collapse was entirely Certificate
Relaxation; the true sign change occurs at $L \approx 0.44$.
Principle~\ref{prin:lb-vs-true} is structurally symmetric with
Principle~\ref{prin:narrative}: the same $\lambda_{lb} \neq \lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{true}}$
gap causes failures in \emph{both} directions --- a false pass (inflated certified
eigenvalue, the omitted-term bug) and a false collapse (deflated certified lower
bound, the relaxation). One rule catches both.

\section{Closeout, and a second instance}
\label{sec:closeout}

Hardening a gate is only credible if the originating artefact is then driven
through it. We regenerated the FP-0.35 certification cleanly: the main
certificate was produced by a genuine four-term recomputation (satisfying
\CtenA{}), and the checker was made to emit mutation-catalog metadata with a
$100\%$ kill rate and a non-empty catalog digest (satisfying \CelevenA{}). The
release dry-run then passed all thirteen conditions with no blockers, and all
17 claims reached the accepted state.

The closeout also produced the paper's sharpest evidence that the conditions
capture a \emph{class} of defect, not a single bug. \CelevenA{} fires on every
attestation that claims recomputation --- and \proofctl{}'s replay tags every
generated attestation \texttt{from\_scratch}. The two sector-lemma attestations
(the main claim's dependencies) therefore also required mutation coverage, but
their checker --- a distinct engine that replays the archimedean base and runs an
exact-rational LDL$^\mathsf{T}$ --- emitted no mutation metadata at all. The
originating fix had addressed only the main claim; the gate correctly refused to
go green until the dependency checkers were given their own honest mutation
catalogs (again $6/6$ kills per sector), built by refactoring the checker's
assembly and judge into shared functions so that the catalog perturbs exactly
what the checker asserts, with no duplicated logic. That a second, independent
instance of the omitted-term blind-spot class surfaced --- and was blocked ---
during routine closeout is the strongest available evidence that \CelevenA{}
encodes a general property rather than patching one certificate.

\begin{remark}[What \texorpdfstring{\protect\proofctl}{proofctl} still does not
guarantee]
The gate verifies that the pinned checker ran, that evidence digests match, that
attestations are untampered, and that obligation and mutation-coverage conditions
hold. It does \emph{not} verify that a checker's mathematics is correct; that
comes from the checker code, its tests, and independent review. Mutation coverage
raises the floor --- a checker must be sensitive to every asserted term --- but a
checker can be sensitive to a term and still integrate it with the wrong
constant. \CtenA{}/\CelevenA{} close two specific, previously-invisible holes;
they do not turn orchestration into a formal kernel.
\end{remark}

\section{Related work and discussion}

Formal kernels (Coq, Isabelle, Lean) give the strongest guarantees but demand the
full proof term; \proofctl{} is complementary, orchestrating \emph{computational}
evidence that such systems could in principle certify. Certificate-passing
compiler verification (CompCert, CertiCoq) shares the architecture in a different
domain. Reproducible-computation tooling (containerised notebooks, workflow
managers) achieves byte-reproducibility but not the proofctl notions of an exact
obligation set, a fail-closed gate, or --- as this paper stresses --- a check
that the reproduced computation is the \emph{claimed} one rather than a copy or a
term-incomplete surrogate.

The transferable lesson is about the \emph{direction} of trust between a proof
and its verifier. A verification system is usually treated as auditing the proof.
The FP-0.35 pilot inverts this: a sufficiently real proof, pushed hard enough,
audits the verifier, and the defects it surfaces are exactly the properties the
verifier's designers did not think to encode. Copy-as-recomputation and
honest-computation-of-the-wrong-quantity are two such properties; there are
surely others. The practical recommendation is to treat every genuine pilot as an
adversary against the gate, and to regard ``a true statement your gate accepted
for the wrong reason'' as the most valuable bug report a proof can file.

\section{Conclusion}

A green release gate certifies the properties its conditions encode, no more. Two
properties --- that a recomputation actually recomputes, and that a checker is
sensitive to every term it claims to verify --- were absent from \proofctl{}'s
gate and violated by a real proof's own certificate, undetectably to digest
binding and schema validation, because the conclusion happened to be correct.
Encoding them as \CtenA{} and \CelevenA{}, and re-driving the corrected pilot
through the hardened gate (where a second instance of the same class was caught),
turned a pair of embarrassing near-misses into durable, reusable conditions. The
episode is a small instance of a general principle worth stating plainly: the
best thing a real computation can do for a verification system is to break it
honestly.
